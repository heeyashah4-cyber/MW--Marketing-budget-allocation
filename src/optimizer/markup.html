Marketing budget optimizer

Ten channels, three years of daily spend and revenue. This app finds where the average rupee performs best, where it stops performing, and what ₹50L/month should do next.

Average ROAS by channel

Simple mean of daily ROAS across all 1,095 days per channel — the figure the allocation formula runs on. The spend-weighted figure (revenue ÷ spend) is shown alongside for reference; the two barely differ, which itself says the data has no big weekday/weekend spend-mix distortion.

Email and SMS lead by a wide margin — but they're also the two channels under a hard spend cap, which is exactly why the naive "give more to whoever has the best ROAS" rule can't just dump the whole budget into them.
02

Diminishing returns — does spending more hurt ROAS?

Each channel's days are split into ten spend deciles; the line tracks average ROAS from the cheapest 10% of days to the most expensive 10%. A flat line means ROAS doesn't depend on how much you spend that day. A downward slope means real diminishing returns. Hover any line to see which channel it belongs to.

Meta Ads is the outlier. Its spend–ROAS correlation is −0.62 — ROAS holds near 3.2× on cheap days and collapses to ~1.2× once daily spend crosses roughly ₹3.3L. Every other channel's correlation sits between −0.06 and +0.04 — statistically indistinguishable from flat. That means for 9 of 10 channels, there's no evidence in three years of data that spending more per day erodes efficiency; Meta Ads is the one channel where pushing harder genuinely burns returns.
03

Timing: weekday vs weekend, and month vs month

Two timing questions the assignment flags: does a channel behave differently on weekends, and are there months where it runs hot or cold.

Google Search is the one real weekday/weekend split: 4.03× on weekdays vs 2.49× on weekends — consistent with B2B/work-related search intent dropping off on Saturdays and Sundays. Every other channel moves by less than 0.2× between weekday and weekend, i.e. within noise.
Every channel roughly doubles in Nov–Dec — the festive/holiday season — and every channel dips in Jun–Jul. Because this shift hits all ten channels together rather than favoring one over another, it's not really a case for reshuffling the channel mix. It's a case for flexing the total monthly budget up in Q4 and down mid-year, which the fixed ₹50L/month brief doesn't currently do.
04

Three-year trend — improving, declining, or flat?

Yearly average ROAS, 2023 through 2025. This is where a channel that's quietly decaying (or an emerging channel) would show up.

Nothing has moved much in three years — the biggest shift is Meta Ads climbing from 2.29× to 2.47× and Affiliate from 5.46× to 5.57×. There's no channel in visible decay and none on a runaway upswing, so the historical average is a fair basis for next month's plan; a channel that had clearly been sliding would need a recency-weighted average instead of a flat 3-year mean.

    The honest takeaway

    Two ways to read "optimal" here, and why the recommendation above deliberately isn't the mathematically-highest one.

    Spreads across all 10 channels in proportion to average ROAS, capped where required.
    Theoretical revenue ceiling
    —
    What you'd get by ranking channels by avg ROAS and filling each to its cap before moving to the next — mathematically higher, but concentrates ~100% of budget into 3 channels.
    Why we don't recommend the ceiling
    It assumes each channel's average ROAS holds unchanged at a completely different spend level, zeroes out 7 channels (brand and competitive risk), and 7 of 10 channels have a spend–ROAS slope statistically indistinguishable from zero — so any model that reallocates hard on that slope is fitting noise, not a real signal.
    The honest takeaway: only Meta Ads shows real, statistically credible diminishing returns (corr = −0.62, n=1,095). Everywhere else, "marginal ROAS" isn't a fitted signal — it's a coin flip dressed up as a regression slope. That's exactly why the optimizer above uses the proportional method rather than chasing the ceiling: it's the allocation that's actually defensible once you account for what the data does and doesn't support. Watch the impact panel above as you nudge allocations toward the ceiling yourself — it'll flag the point where you're pushing a channel past the spend levels its own history actually supports.
